The profile problem

THE PERFECT DATING PROFILE: EVERYTHING WE KNOW MIGHT BE WRONG.

A million likes look nice on a graph. They're considerably less useful than one person who actually wants to meet you.

Author: Hoss the AICo-author: Hoss the crankerNovember 5, 2026OgTrends / Research & ideas

Humanity has spent a few thousand years trying to figure out how to be irresistible. Poetry, peacock feathers, perfume, carefully selected photographs (or clay tablet carvings) of people holding fish. We have never actually found perfection in attraction. That hasn't stopped us from trying, and it sure as hell won't stop OgTrends. But before we start promising the perfect dating profile, there's a small question to settle: perfect at what?

Getting looked at? Getting messages? Getting twenty-seven versions of “hey beautiful”? Our definition is considerably less romantic on paper and considerably more romantic in practice: the best profile is the one that gets the right people talking, gets them talking back, and eventually gets them together. A profile that starts 40 conversations and leads to 12 dates might be doing more good than one that starts 100 and produces two. Those numbers are illustrative, not OG Cupid results. But that's the point of the whole undertaking. We'd rather have you meet somebody you like than keep you on a website indefinitely, admiring your unread messages like a little museum of disappointment.

Two generations walk into a website

Here's where OG Cupid gets weird in the best possible way. We're expecting at least two sorts of visitors. First, thirty- and fortysomethings who remember when a dating website felt like a place: profiles worth reading, bizarre quizzes, journals, forums, and people you could get to know without immediately swiping them into the void. Second, people who've never really experienced a major social platform that wasn't organized around opaque recommendation feeds, advertising pressure, relentless data collection, and outrage or anxiety repackaged as “engagement.” For the first group, OG Cupid might feel like coming home. For the second, it might feel like discovering the internet once had more than five sites.

And what a wonderful experiment that second group presents. How will people raised in the swipe era behave when they can browse somebody's entire profile, decide whom to approach, and send a first message without completing the two-person “we both swiped yes” ceremony? Will they write thoughtful introductions? Will they freeze in horror at the responsibility? Will somebody send an unsolicited dog picture and accidentally get married? We don't know. Open messaging would need sensible anti-spam tools, boundaries, and blocking, not a free pass for harassment, but the ability to initiate a conversation without an algorithm granting an audience is a social dynamic worth studying all by itself.

Was OkCupid the original social network? No. SixDegrees, Friendster, and MySpace would all like a word, and OkCupid didn't arrive until 2004. But it absolutely belongs in the early history of profile-driven social communities. Its tests, member-created quizzes, journals, public discussions, compatibility questions, and gloriously overstuffed profiles made it more than a dating catalog. It wasn't a magical ad-free utopia, and it had algorithms of its own. What we miss is that people could browse, search, read, and choose, instead of having every encounter flattened into whatever a feed decided to serve them next. That deserves a place in internet history. [8, 9]

THE QUESTION UNDERNEATH ALL THIS: Did we forget how to write interesting profiles, or did the platforms get worse at letting us appreciate them?

Old OkTrends was asking the right question

In 2010, Christian Rudder and the original OkTrends team hand-tagged 7,140 profile photographs and compared how different kinds of pictures performed in actual messaging. Smiling wasn't universally better. Looking straight at the camera wasn't universally better. And photographs that inspired an avalanche of attention didn't necessarily inspire the kind of back-and-forth that makes somebody want to meet you. The research came from selected big-city users ages 18–32, so don't take it as a commandment handed down from Mount Tinder. But it does seem oddly prophetic: a detailed examination of the difference between being looked at and being worth talking to, before the swipe era reared its ugly head. [1]

We could call that a victory for boobs and close the laptop. Except Rudder kept digging. He examined whether an incoming message went unanswered or blossomed into an actual conversation, and argued that being conversation-worthy mattered independently of looking sexy. This is exactly the distinction we're after. There's no law requiring a good profile to be universally appealing. A photo of your homemade dinosaur costume might impress fewer people than a professionally lit glamour shot, but the person who asks how you built the tail may already be someone worth talking to. That's our inference, not a statistic from the dinosaur-costume industry.

Another marvelous relic: in 2009 the original crew analyzed more than half a million opening messages. About 32% got a reply. Messages containing “haha” had a 45% reply rate, “lol” 41%, and “please” 22%. But their findings weren't just an endorsement of laughing maniacally into a stranger's inbox. Messages that referenced specific interests and showed evidence somebody had actually read a profile also performed well. None of this proves adding a word causes a reply; the researchers observed existing conversations, not a randomized vocabulary experiment. You shouldn't open with “haha please.” You should open with unsolicited dog pics. Preferably of an actual dog. [2, 3]

Of course, a profile can be engineered to attract attention and still leave the person behind it awfully hard to locate. In “The Big Lies People Tell In Online Dating,” OkTrends found that 12% of the most attractive-looking uploaded photos were more than three years old, compared with 5% of average-looking photos. That's not proof anybody was intentionally lying. But it illustrates how a winning picture can drift away from the human who eventually turns up for dinner. [4]

Now count the people who escaped the app

Fast-forward to a 2019 paper studying historical Hinge activity: 421,690,471 potential pairings, 2,148,947 conversations, and 508,989 “effective matches,” defined as an exchange of contact information. That's closer to the actual point of online dating than counting swipes, but it's still not a verified first date. The researchers observed relationships between some shared characteristics, goals, and effective matches; they did not prove that making a profile look a particular way causes more people to meet. [5]

And then we run headlong into the part no amount of old data can settle. The best profile for whom? The reader's personality, relationship goals, intelligence, humor, and tolerance for someone who owns fourteen reptiles might matter as much as the writer's charms. We want to study measured cognitive ability separately from how intelligent someone's writing sounds, and compare outcomes across different audiences. A 2022 study suggests original, specific writing can improve first impressions and stated willingness to date, but it didn't measure actual dates. And a 2017 machine-learning study found that even mountains of preferences and traits struggled to predict the unique spark between two particular speed daters. Chemistry continues to refuse to file the proper paperwork. [6, 7]

A dating site that actually wants you to leave

We may never know the best way to market yourself in a dating profile. Not because the research is worthless, but because the environment changes, the people change, and the entire sales-pitch idea might be part of the problem. A brilliant 2010 profile might languish on a 2026 swipe app. A supposedly terrible profile might attract exactly one perfect weirdo on a website where people still get to read. The slow deconstruction of online dating has demonstrated a nasty possibility: a platform can become remarkably efficient at generating activity without becoming particularly good at getting people together. Our priority is, and always will be, getting people together.

So we'll use old research the way it deserves to be used: as a collection of clues, not a recipe for algorithmically approved love. If OG Cupid launches and enough members consent to participate, we'd like to compare truthful versions of profiles under fair exposure conditions, measure reciprocal conversations, and ask members voluntarily whether they actually met. We'll see whether different kinds of people behave differently when given control of discovery and the freedom to message first. We'll never pretend an exchanged phone number is a marriage certificate, or snoop through private off-site conversations to manufacture a success metric.

Maybe we reconstruct the conditions that once made interesting profiles matter. Maybe we discover entirely new rules. Maybe we never find perfection at all. Which brings us to the possibility we ought to admit out loud: we don't know. The data are old, stale, conditional, or plain inapplicable to this grand experiment and holy revival, complete with speaking in tongues and snake handling for a few lucky dudes. Hell, we may learn that all we accomplished was creating a place where people can enjoy themselves again. We'd call that a pretty decent start.

We intend to make the experiment worth showing up for. Some people will find dates. Some will find friends. Some will make quizzes about finding their ideal werewolf to fuck. Some will simply discover that an internet community can be something other than a five-app rotation of scrolling and irritation. We're all gonna have fun either way, god damnit.
