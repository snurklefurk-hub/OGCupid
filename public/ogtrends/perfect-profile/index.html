The profile problem

THE PERFECT DATING PROFILE: EVERYTHING WE KNOW MIGHT BE WRONG.

A million likes look nice on a graph. They're considerably less useful than one person who actually wants to meet you.

Author: Hoss the AICo-author: Hoss the crankerNovember 5, 2026OgTrends / Research & ideas

Dating advice has one universal problem: it treats “success” as if everybody agrees on what it means. More views? More matches? More strangers opening with “hey”? Here at OgTrends, we're proposing a ruder measure: how many real conversations start, move off the website, and become dates both people actually wanted. A profile that starts 40 chats and leads to 12 dates may be doing better than one that starts 100 chats and leads to two. Those numbers are an illustration, not a finding.

That means studying the whole funnel, not just the pretty part: eligible profile views, new conversations, reciprocal conversations, off-site contact or plans, confirmed first dates, and whether both people wanted another. Each stage answers a different question. A gorgeous profile might attract attention; an honest one might turn away a few people before wasting three weeks of their lives.

The original OkTrends crew understood this. In a 2010 analysis of 7,140 OkCupid users, Christian Rudder and company examined photograph choices alongside actual messaging behavior. Their provocative observation: being conversation-worthy could matter more than simply being sexy. But their outcomes weren't verified dates, the sample was restricted, and the data came from a dating internet that no longer exists. [1]

Then there's the writing. In a 2022 study, researchers began with 31,163 authentic dating-profile texts, selected 308 for detailed analysis, and found that more original descriptions were associated with more attractive impressions and greater stated willingness to date. Concrete self-disclosure and distinctive phrasing helped make writing feel original. Important footnote: the researchers measured people's impressions, not whether those people eventually met for coffee. [2]

421 million chances. How many went somewhere?

A 2019 analysis of historical Hinge data finally gets much closer to what we care about. Across 421,690,471 potential pairings, the researchers counted 2,148,947 conversations and 508,989 “effective matches,” defined as an exchange of contact information. That's a meaningful step toward leaving the app—but not proof of an actual meeting. These are outcomes from that older Hinge population, not performance benchmarks for OG Cupid. [3]

The same study found that shared characteristics—such as stated relationship goals and some experiences—often lined up with effective matches. One exception involved pairings between two introverts, who rarely reached that contact-exchange threshold. This is a useful warning against the idea that you can optimize a profile for “everybody”: the audience matters. It is also observational evidence, not proof that rewriting a sentence causes more phone-number exchanges. [3]

So here's a question we really want to test: does intellectual style work differently for different readers? Perhaps a witty, abstract profile delights a particular audience and bores another. We could study a profile owner's measured cognitive ability, the reader's ability, and the perceived intelligence of the writing separately. Low, medium, high, and exceptionally high ability are possible reporting groups; they aren't four preordained kinds of romantic human. We have no credible evidence yet that “writing to someone's IQ” improves date conversion. That's a hypothesis, not a result.

There are limits to the whole compatibility dream, too. In two 2017 speed-dating studies, researchers used over 100 self-reported traits and preferences and still couldn't predict the unique spark between particular pairs beyond broad tendencies to desire or be desired. Matching can help people find promising candidates; it cannot currently promise chemistry. [4]

And time changes the experiment. A 2010 desktop browser, a 2015 mobile app, and a 2026 swipe feed don't show people the same information or reward the same decisions. Historic findings about humor or self-disclosure may travel surprisingly well; historic message-conversion rates probably don't. We're especially interested in whether bringing back substantial profiles and user-controlled browsing gives readers more room to notice the very traits the swipe economy buried.

Our proposed test is straightforward: consenting members try randomized versions of their own profiles, while eligibility, exposure, photos, and relevant audience factors are held as steady as possible. We track reciprocal conversations and voluntary, privacy-respecting reports of off-site transitions and dates; we don't spy on people's phones. We'd then ask whether effects differ by personality, communication style, intellectual style, relationship intent, and who is doing the reading.

Our wager: the best dating profile won't be the one that makes the largest number of strangers think you're acceptable. It'll be the one that helps the right strangers recognize you—and eventually leave the damn website together. The research hasn't proven that yet. We'd rather find out than sell everybody another set of magic words.
