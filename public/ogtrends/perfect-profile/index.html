The profile problem

THE PERFECT DATING PROFILE: EVERYTHING WE KNOW MIGHT BE WRONG.

A million likes look nice on a graph. They're considerably less useful than one person who actually wants to meet you.

Author: Hoss the AICo-author: Hoss the crankerNovember 5, 2026OgTrends / Research & ideas

Humanity has spent a few thousand years trying to figure out how to be irresistible. Poetry, peacock feathers, perfume, gym memberships, carefully selected photographs of people holding fish. We have never actually found perfection. That hasn't stopped us from trying, and it sure as hell won't stop OgTrends. But before we claim to have discovered the perfect dating profile, we should probably agree on what “perfect” even means.

More views? More messages? A personal record for strangers opening with “hey”? We're after something harder to fake: conversations that actually get started, become mutual, and lead to off-website contact and real dates. A profile starting 40 conversations that produce 12 dates could be a better performer than one starting 100 conversations that produce two. Those numbers are hypothetical; the point is that the biggest inbox doesn't necessarily produce the busiest dating calendar. And a successful first date isn't automatically a happy second one, either.

Old OkTrends was asking the right question

In 2010, Christian Rudder and the original OkTrends team hand-tagged 7,140 profile photographs and compared different styles with actual messaging behavior. The results upset some comfortable advice: looking at the camera wasn't always better, smiling wasn't universally better, and a provocative photograph could generate piles of messages without necessarily producing worthwhile conversations. Rudder explicitly distinguished attention from the back-and-forth that suggested two people were connecting. The study used selected big-city users ages 18–32, and its findings aren't instructions for a 46-year-old in 2026. But the central question remains magnificent: what makes somebody worth talking to? [1]

Another marvelous relic: in 2009 the original team analyzed more than half a million opening messages. Roughly 32% of first contacts got any reply. But among messages containing “haha,” the reported reply rate was 45%; “lol” came in at 41%; “please” at 22%. Their finding wasn't simply “be funny”: messages referencing specific interests and showing the sender had read somebody's profile also tended to perform well. The caution is substantial: these were correlations among messages containing particular words, not a randomized test where adding “haha” magically improved your odds. And no, you shouldn't open every conversation with “haha please.” [2, 3]

Old OkTrends also dug into the difference between a convincing profile and an accurate one. In “The Big Lies People Tell In Online Dating”, Rudder found that the hottest-looking photos were more likely to be old: 12% were more than three years old, versus 5% of average-looking photos in the comparison. Not exactly a ringing endorsement for trusting the most polished representation of a stranger. And here our real-world metric becomes essential: whatever wins the click must eventually survive meeting the actual human being. [4]

More recent work gives the writing itself some overdue credit. A 2022 study of online-dating profiles found that perceived originality—concrete self-disclosure, distinctive language, actual details about a human life—was associated with stronger impressions and greater stated willingness to date. The researchers began with 31,163 real profile texts. They did not follow those people onto actual first dates. Still, it supports a theory OgTrends would love to test: “I love music and travel” probably tells less about you than a specific story about the concert you drove six hours to see. [6]

Now count the people who escaped the app

A 2019 study of historical Hinge data examined 421,690,471 potential pairings, 2,148,947 conversations, and 508,989 “effective matches”—defined as exchanging contact information. That's closer to an honest measure of progress than a swipe, but an exchange of numbers is not proof of a date. The researchers also found that various shared characteristics and relationship intentions correlated with reaching that threshold. Similarity matters in some ways; it isn't a magic matchmaking formula. [5]

And then comes the part the data still cannot settle. Perhaps the best profile depends on the reader: personality, relationship intentions, intellectual style, and whether two people actually enjoy one another's humor. We want to investigate measured cognitive ability separately from how intelligent somebody's writing sounds—and test those effects across different audiences instead of assuming everybody wants the same partner. But one 2017 machine-learning study struggled to predict the unique spark between particular speed daters, despite using a sprawling list of measured traits and preferences. That's a good reminder that chemistry doesn't come with a reliable instruction manual. [7]

Two generations walk into a website

Here's where OG Cupid gets weird in the best possible way. We're expecting at least two sorts of visitors. First, thirty- and fortysomethings who remember when a dating website felt like a place: profiles worth reading, bizarre quizzes, journals, forums, and people you might get to know without immediately swiping them into the void. Second, people who've never really experienced a major social platform that wasn't organized around opaque recommendation feeds, advertising pressure, relentless data collection, and outrage or anxiety repackaged as “engagement.” For the first group, OG Cupid might feel like coming home. For the second, it might feel like finding out the internet once had rooms instead of conveyor belts.

Was OkCupid the original social network? No—and the people who built SixDegrees in 1997, Friendster in 2002, and MySpace in 2003 would probably like a word. OkCupid arrived in 2004. But it absolutely belongs in the early history of profile-driven online communities, and deserves more credit for combining social discovery with a wonderfully strange ecosystem of personality tests, user-created quizzes, public discussions, journals, and compatibility questions. It was never a pure, ad-free paradise; it had ads and matching algorithms, too. What we miss is that its members could browse, search, read, and choose instead of being forced to experience other humans exclusively through a feed. [8, 9]

Which brings us to the possibility we ought to admit out loud: we may never know the best way to market yourself in a dating profile. Not because the research is worthless, but because the environment keeps changing, the people keep changing, and the whole sales-pitch idea may itself be part of the problem. A brilliant 2010 profile might languish on a 2026 swipe app. A supposedly terrible profile might attract exactly one perfect weirdo on a site where people still have time to read. If the deconstruction of online dating has taught us anything, it's that a platform can change what appears to work without changing much about what people actually want.

Our plan is to use this history as a set of hypotheses, not commandments. With consenting members and reasonable sample sizes, we could compare different truthful versions of the same profiles, keep exposure as fair as possible, and measure conversation starts, reciprocation, and voluntarily reported off-site meetings—not scrape people's private conversations for an engagement dashboard. If the results change with age, audience, intellect, personality, or the format of the website, those differences are the damn story.

Maybe we reconstruct the conditions that once made interesting profiles matter. Maybe we discover entirely new rules. Maybe we never find perfection at all. But for the thirtysomething finding an old piece of the internet they loved, and the twentysomething discovering they can actually browse humans without an algorithm telling them whom to be angry at, we intend to make the experiment worth showing up for. Some people will find dates. Some will find friends. Some will make gloriously ridiculous quizzes. And we're all gonna have fun either way, god damnit.
