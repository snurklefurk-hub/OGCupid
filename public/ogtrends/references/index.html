A proposal, not a launched feature

YOUR EX HAS ENTERED THE CHAT.

What if a dating profile came with references—and the people writing them had reputations of their own?

Author: Hoss the AICo-author: Hoss the crankerNovember 5, 2026OgTrends / Research & ideas

Every dating profile is a sales pitch written by the person being sold. “I'm easygoing.” “I communicate well.” “No drama.” Marvelous. Now imagine a job interview where the candidate got to write every reference, approve every compliment, and quietly destroy every criticism. That's a great system for producing flattering paragraphs; it's a less convincing system for producing truth.

There is research behind our suspicion. In a 2016 experiment with 316 online daters, Crystal Wotipka and Andrew High studied warranting: information that makes claims in a profile easier to trust or corroborate. Highly self-promotional profiles tended to backfire, while stronger warranting increased trust, especially when people weren't bragging. Their manipulated profiles used corroborating details and links, not ex-partner testimonials, and measured impressions and intentions rather than actual dates. [1]

Friends can tell us things a person would never say about themselves. An ex might know something different: what they're like when the electricity bill is late, when they're hurt, or when somebody has to apologize first. Neither source is automatically honest or correct. But a nuanced account—“reliable, funny, and stubborn as hell when he's wrong”—might reveal more than another three paragraphs of “I'm a great catch.”

Third-party opinions can move romantic choices. A 2012 virtual-dating experiment found that friends' purported judgments influenced liking and predicted dating choices in the task; parental opinions were less directly predictive. That doesn't demonstrate that publicly posted references lead to more real-world dates. It does suggest that opinions from people around a potential partner are not just decorative text. [2]

And we're not alone on this trail. In July 2026, Hinge introduced “Friend's Take,” allowing outside contributors to offer testimonials, with users choosing up to three for display. Their contributors don't even need Hinge accounts. It's a useful idea, but because owners choose the flattering three, what viewers see is still a curated selection. As far as we've verified, no published outcome data yet show whether that feature increases actual dates. [3]

Our proposed version: receipts, not ratings

Here's the OG Cupid proposition: only members may write references, and they must have earned OG Status. No anonymous potshots from a throwaway link. Writers' profiles show their account history and participation, and readers can follow through to see how they've treated other people. OG Status would be a participation gate, not a certification that someone tells the truth; it can be earned on day one, so the system would still need safeguards against coordinated abuse.

The trick is making both directions visible. A spiteful ex doesn't get to materialize, write one venomous review, and disappear without leaving context. You can inspect the writer's actual profile, how long they've participated, the references they've received, and the ones they've written. None of that proves a negative reference false or a positive one true. It does mean readers can examine credibility rather than swallowing a star rating whole.

But a public reference system could be weaponized. A real ex can tell lies; a newer member can tell the truth; a whole friend group can coordinate. Our working rule would be that ordinary negative opinion isn't removable just because it's unflattering, but harassment, private intimate disclosures, threats, identifying details, and serious unverified allegations can't become a public revenge board. Recipients need a right to reply and appeal; safety reports need a separate confidential channel. Relationship labels should indicate whether both parties confirmed them, not quietly imply independent verification.

Most importantly, we would never call references a safety certification or punish people who have none. Not everyone wants to recruit their exes onto a dating website; plenty of honest people would rather eat a shoe. References would be optional, displayed prominently only when the owner chooses, and subject to fair rules for authors and recipients alike. A missing reference is not a character defect.

And because we're OgTrends, we don't get to stop at “sounds cool.” Once it's safe to test, we'd compare otherwise similar profiles displaying no references, friend references, ex references, and balanced references—with consent and consistent exposure—and measure trust, conversations, voluntarily reported off-site transitions, and dates. Our hypothesis is that nuanced third-party observations may improve the quality of matches even if they reduce the raw volume of attention. That's a hypothesis. We haven't tested it yet.

The ambition is modest and ridiculous at the same time: let people speak for themselves, then give the community a way to add context. Less résumé. More actual human. And absolutely no Yelp rating for boyfriends.
